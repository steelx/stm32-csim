00 / Setup · 45–75 minutes

Make the bench predictable.

Board assumption: header names below refer to a NUCLEO-F446RE, MB1136 with onboard ST-LINK. Check the printed board name. “STM32F446RE” identifies the MCU, not a universal board layout. For another board, first map PA5, PA10, 3V3 and GND using its own schematic; do not use Nucleo header positions.

Gather nowOptional / verify firstKeep aside
Nucleo, USB data cable, breadboard, jumpers; red LED; 1 kΩ and 10 kΩ resistors; button; LA1010 and probes.Multimeter; 100 nF ceramic and 1 µF capacitor rated ≥6.3 V; known potentiometer (about 10 kΩ is convenient); identified NPN and 3.3 V-compatible N-MOSFET; 100 kΩ resistors.Motors, relay, display modules and other powered boards. They are not needed for this module.

Examples assume ordinary ≥0.125 W resistors and a red LED with about 2.0 V forward drop at low current. Your LED may differ. If values are missing, combine resistors using lesson 1’s formulas and recalculate. Do not silently substitute unidentified transistors. A multimeter is useful but not listed in your inventory: every meter-dependent exercise includes a calculation alternative.

Understand the breadboard

On a typical breadboard, each group of five holes on one side of the center gap shares a conductor. The opposite group does not. Power rails may be split halfway and the two sides are usually separate. Confirm your board with an unpowered continuity test or its documented internal layout. Put each LED leg in a different connected group. A four-leg tactile switch has two internally connected pairs: pressing joins them. Identify those pairs instead of guessing from appearance.

Bench Guardrail

Supply: ST-LINK USB in the documented USB-power jumper configuration; only 3V3 and GND for external circuits. Rewire: unplug USB and disconnect the analyzer first. Budget: below 3 mA for an external LED and below 10 mA total external-circuit current; these are conservative course targets, not maximum ratings. Keep disconnected: motors, relay coils, 5 V logic, mains and extra supplies.

Prepare the firmware once

  1. Install STM32CubeIDE from ST if needed. Create an STM32 C project for STM32F446RETx, choosing Empty where offered. Keep the provided startup code, linker script, CMSIS headers and system_stm32f4xx.c. Wizard wording varies by version.
  2. Replace the project’s Core/Src/main.c (or Src/main.c) with lab-main.c. Include it exactly once. The compiler must define STM32F446xx and include the CMSIS directories. In a CubeMX-generated project, replace the existing main instead of adding another.
  3. Leave LAB_MODE at 0 and build. A missing device header means you need to correct the selected device or CMSIS paths; do not copy headers from another MCU.
  4. Connect the bare Nucleo using a USB data cable. Use its onboard ST-LINK debug configuration, download the program, and Resume if execution stops at main. LD2 should toggle. If ST-LINK is missing, inspect the cable, drivers and documented jumper positions.
  5. After every mode change, rebuild and download. Disconnect power before adding wiring.

This companion runs on the real board. The repository’s current simulator only models a small PA5 output subset; it cannot execute the companion’s input, pull and slew register accesses or simulate RC curves/current. You can separately use the existing simulator blink example to study output registers. Its timing is not a physical measurement.

Three modes, one file

LAB_MODE 0: blink PA5 using an uncalibrated delay.
LAB_MODE 1: active-low PA10 button controls PA5; no debounce.
LAB_MODE 2: hold PA5 high for steady measurements.

The code enables GPIOA’s clock, preloads a low output, configures PA5 as push-pull and PA10 as an input without internal pulls. BSRR sets pins with its lower half and resets them with its upper half. IDR reads inputs. Module 5 will unpack each register; here the firmware is a bench instrument. Delay depends on compiler and clock settings, so no exact blink frequency is promised.

Connect the LA1010

  1. Use Kingst’s software and model-specific guide. Configure the used leads as digital inputs; leave stimulus/PWM outputs disconnected. An analyzer output is not a power supply.
  2. With power off, connect LA GND to Nucleo GND and input CH0 to D13/PA5. A channel lead alone is insufficient.
  3. Run mode 0. Choose a 3.3 V CMOS-compatible threshold, or near 1.65 V if your software/model allows an adjustable threshold. Check the manual rather than assuming the option exists.
  4. Enable only needed channels. Start near 100 kS/s with 1–2 seconds of capture. Use CH0 rising-edge trigger if available. Adjust duration/sample rate to the device’s supported memory settings if a complete pulse does not fit.
  5. Measure high duration, low duration and period. Frequency = 1 / period. A debugger breakpoint freezes the output.
Bench Guardrail

LA1010: digital levels and transition times. DMM: steady voltage. Oscilloscope with suitable probes: fast analog waveforms, including ripple and ramp shape. The analyzer does not measure amplitude or current, and a calculated prediction is not measured data.

01 / Resistors, part 1 · 35–50 minutes · Build

Choose current before choosing a resistor.

Physical Intuition / Mental Model

Voltage provides the push, but a complete path and its resistance determine how much charge flows each second. The same current passes through a series LED and resistor; the resistor turns its share of the electrical energy into heat while protecting the LED.

Voltage is a difference in electric potential between two nodes. Current is the rate of charge flow through a complete path. Start by tracing that path: series components carry the same current, and at a junction the incoming currents add up to the outgoing currents.

VR = I × R

VR: voltage across the resistor (V); I: current through it (A); R: resistance (Ω).

The voltage belongs to a pair of nodes; the current belongs to a branch. Name both before substituting values.

Worked design: a red indicator near 1 mA

First allocate the voltage, then choose the current, then check the heat. Use a 3.3 V high output and an assumed 2.0 V LED drop for this first estimate.

VR = VS − VF
= 3.3 V − 2.0 V = 1.3 V

VR: resistor voltage (V); VS: source voltage (V); VF: LED forward drop (V).

The LED and resistor share the available voltage.

R = VR / Itarget
= 1.3 V / 0.001 A = 1.3 kΩ

R: required resistance (Ω); VR: resistor voltage (V); Itarget: chosen LED current (A).

A 1 kΩ part gives about 1.3 mA; a 2.2 kΩ part gives about 0.59 mA. These are low-current indicators, not brightness tests.

PR = VRI = I²R = VR² / R
= (1.3 V)² / 1000 Ω = 1.69 mW

PR: resistor heating power (W); VR: voltage across this resistor (V); I: its current (A); R: its resistance (Ω).

This is comfortably below a 0.125 W rating. Using the entire 3.3 V in this power calculation would assign the LED’s voltage drop to the resistor.

Lab 1 · Your first external load

Parts: red LED, 1 kΩ resistor and jumpers. Identify anode/cathode from the datasheet or diode test. Long leg often means anode and flat rim often means cathode, but clipped leads and packages vary.

  1. With power off, connect D13 → resistor → LED anode; LED cathode → GND. Each connection needs a distinct breadboard node.
  2. Run mode 0. The external LED should follow LD2. Probe D13 with CH0, not the LED cathode.
  3. Power off and substitute 2.2 kΩ if available. Predict the new current before comparing brightness. The logic waveform should remain essentially unchanged.
  4. Optional DMM: use mode 2 and measure voltage across the resistor. Divide that reading by its resistance to estimate current, as in the worked example.

Expected: regular on/off behavior. A dark external LED points to its polarity or current path; if both LEDs are dark, remove the breadboard and verify firmware on LD2 alone.

Bench Guardrail

Inspect polarity with power disconnected. Use the DMM in voltage mode across the resistor; a current-mode meter placed across a supply would short it. The LA1010 confirms switching times, while resistor voltage plus resistance gives the current estimate.

Try the design calculation

Ideal 3.3 V source; ignores output droop and the separate onboard LD2 branch.

Series, parallel and tolerance

Rseries = R₁ + R₂
= 1 kΩ + 1 kΩ = 2 kΩ

Rseries: equivalent series resistance (Ω); R₁, R₂: the two individual resistances (Ω).

The same current passes through both resistors; their voltage drops add.

Rparallel = R₁R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)
= (1 kΩ × 1 kΩ) / (2 kΩ) = 500 Ω

Rparallel: equivalent parallel resistance (Ω); R₁, R₂: individual resistances (Ω).

Both resistors see the same voltage; the second branch provides another path for current.

A 1 kΩ ±5% part can be 950–1050 Ω. Highest supply, lowest LED forward drop and lowest resistance produce the highest LED current. Design for that combination, not only nominal values.

Check yourself: a 1 kΩ resistor has 1.2 V across it. Current and power?

I = VR/R = 1.2 V / 1000 Ω = 1.2 mA
PR = VR²/R = (1.2 V)² / 1000 Ω = 1.44 mW

I: resistor current (A); VR: measured resistor voltage (V); R: resistance (Ω); PR: resistor heating power (W).

Common Bench Trap

Using the supply voltage instead of the voltage across the resistor gives the wrong current and heat estimate. Put the DMM probes on the resistor’s two ends in voltage mode, and convert 1 kΩ to 1000 Ω before calculating.

02 / Resistors, part 2 · 40–55 minutes · Build

Give every input a defined voltage.

Physical Intuition / Mental Model

Two resistors divide a supply voltage because the same current creates a voltage drop across each one. Connecting something to their midpoint creates another current path, so the voltage you predicted for an unloaded divider can move.

Begin with no load attached to the midpoint. Both resistors carry the same current, so their resistance ratio sets the share of the supply that appears between the midpoint and ground.

Vout = Vin × Rbottom / (Rtop + Rbottom)
= 3.3 V × 10 kΩ / 20 kΩ = 1.65 V

Vin: supply relative to GND (V); Vout: midpoint relative to GND (V); Rtop: supply-to-midpoint resistance (Ω); Rbottom: midpoint-to-ground resistance (Ω).

This ratio assumes the midpoint is unloaded.

I = Vin / (Rtop + Rbottom)
= 3.3 V / 20,000 Ω = 165 µA

I: current through the unloaded series pair (A); Vin: supply voltage (V); Rtop, Rbottom: divider resistances (Ω).

The divider consumes current even when nothing is connected to its midpoint.

Now add a 10 kΩ load from Vout to GND. It sits in parallel with the lower resistor: the new current path changes the divider itself.

Rlower = Rbottom ∥ Rload
= 10 kΩ ∥ 10 kΩ = 5 kΩ
Vout,loaded = 3.3 V × 5 kΩ / (10 kΩ + 5 kΩ) = 1.1 V

Rlower: effective lower resistance (Ω); Rbottom: original lower resistor (Ω); Rload: added load resistance (Ω); ∥: parallel combination; Vout,loaded: loaded midpoint voltage (V).

Loading reduces the midpoint from 1.65 V to 1.1 V.

Rout = Rtop ∥ Rbottom
= 10 kΩ ∥ 10 kΩ = 5 kΩ

Rout: original divider’s Thévenin output resistance (Ω), with an ideal supply; Rtop, Rbottom: its resistors (Ω); ∥: parallel combination.

This is a measure of how easily a load disturbs the output; it will also matter for ADC sampling.

Lab 2A · Adjustable voltage

  1. Build the divider and predict the midpoint. If you have a DMM, measure before and after adding the 10 kΩ load. Without one, do both calculations; a logic analyzer cannot verify 1.65 V.
  2. For a known potentiometer, connect the two track-end terminals to 3V3 and GND and use the wiper as Vout. Identify the wiper from its datasheet or unpowered resistance measurements; physical middle is common, not guaranteed.
  3. Check the potentiometer’s total track resistance and power rating. For a 10 kΩ track, use the calculation below.
  4. Rotate and observe with a DMM. If the pot is unidentified or no meter is available, use the paper route.

Ptrack = Vin² / Rtrack
= (3.3 V)² / 10,000 Ω = 1.09 mW

Ptrack: power in the complete track (W); Vin: voltage across its ends (V); Rtrack: end-to-end track resistance (Ω).

The unloaded wiper position changes the output voltage, not the resistance of the full track.

Bench Guardrail

Leave A0 and all other MCU pins disconnected from Vout in this lesson. A DMM can measure the midpoint; the LA1010 cannot verify its analog voltage. A low-resistance potentiometer can overload the rail, so identify its resistance and rating before connecting it.

Lab 2B · An active-low button

A GPIO input is high impedance. An unconnected input floats and may respond to noise. A pull-up weakly holds it high; a switch can override that state to ground.

  1. Disconnect USB. Identify the switch pairs, then wire the circuit above. Use the combined capstone layout to keep the LED. The individual lab diagrams reuse rows; do not stack them on the same breadboard unchanged.
  2. Set LAB_MODE 1, rebuild and download. Released: PA10 high and LED off. Pressed: PA10 low and LED on.
  3. Capture CH0 = PA5 and CH1 = PA10. Start near 1 MS/s and around 100 ms capture, triggering on CH1 falling. Adapt to supported memory/rates.
  4. Zoom into press and release. You may see several transitions: mechanical contact bounce. The firmware intentionally follows these. A clean press does not prove all presses are clean.

Expected: released = 1, pressed = 0. Always low suggests wrong switch pairing or a shorted node. Always high suggests an open ground/switch path.

Ipressed = V3V3 / Rpull-up
= 3.3 V / 10,000 Ω = 0.33 mA

Ipressed: pull-up branch current with the switch closed (A); V3V3: STM32 board rail voltage (V); Rpull-up: external pull-up resistance (Ω).

Closing the switch pulls the high-impedance input low while the resistor limits supply current.

Bench Guardrail

Keep the 10 kΩ pull-up between 3V3 and BUTTON. Never connect the switch directly between a driven output and ground; the button here goes to the PA10 input.

Check yourself: why use a resistor rather than connecting the input node directly to 3V3?

When pressed, a direct connection would short 3V3 to ground. The 10 kΩ resistor limits this current to 0.33 mA while still defining a high level for the high-impedance input.

Common Bench Trap

Replacing the pull-up with a jumper makes a button press a supply short. Keep the 10 kΩ in the supply-to-BUTTON path, verify the switch’s internal pairs with power off, then expect high released and low pressed.

03 / Capacitors · 40–60 minutes · Build or calculate

A capacitor makes voltage take time.

Physical Intuition / Mental Model

A capacitor behaves like a small charge reservoir: its voltage rises only as charge accumulates. A resistor limits how quickly that charge can arrive or leave, making a sudden input change become a gradual voltage change.

At ideal DC steady state a capacitor passes no current; during a change it accepts or releases charge. Real parts also have leakage and parasitic resistance and inductance. Small bypass capacitors provide fast local current, while bulk capacitors support slower changes.

Q = CV
i = C × dV/dt

Q: stored charge (C, coulombs); C: capacitance (F, farads); V: capacitor voltage (V); i: current entering its positive terminal (A); dV/dt: voltage change per second (V/s).

A faster voltage change requires more current. Here the symbol C means capacitance; the unit C means coulombs.

τ = RC
VC(t) = VS × (1 − e−t/τ)

τ: time constant (s); R: series resistance (Ω); C: capacitance (F); t: elapsed time (s); VC(t): capacitor voltage at time t (V); VS: final step voltage (V); e: exponential base (dimensionless).

For a capacitor initially at 0 V, charging through R reaches 63.2% of the step after τ and 99.3% after 5τ.

VC(t) = Vinitial × e−t/τ

VC(t): capacitor voltage (V); Vinitial: voltage at the start of discharge (V); t: elapsed time (s); τ = RC: discharge time constant (s); e: exponential base (dimensionless).

For discharge through R toward 0 V, the same time constant describes how quickly voltage falls.

Worked example

τ = RC = 10,000 Ω × 0.000001 F = 0.010 s
VC(τ) = 3.3 V × (1 − e−1) ≈ 2.09 V

τ: time constant (s); R: series resistance (Ω); C: capacitance (F); VC(τ): voltage after one time constant (V); e: exponential base (dimensionless).

First convert kΩ and µF to Ω and F; the product is seconds.

tcross = −RC × ln(1 − Vthreshold/VS)
= −10 ms × ln(1 − 1.65/3.3) ≈ 6.93 ms

tcross: threshold-crossing time (s); R: resistance (Ω); C: capacitance (F); Vthreshold: assumed digital threshold (V); VS: step voltage (V); ln: natural logarithm of a dimensionless ratio.

This assumes charging starts at 0 V and the threshold is exactly 1.65 V. Threshold accuracy, loading and component tolerance change the real result.

Lab 3 · Measure a threshold delay

  1. Remove the button circuit. With power off, fit 10 kΩ and 1 µF as shown. Do not connect the capacitor directly to PA5; the resistor limits initial charging current to about 0.33 mA.
  2. For an electrolytic, verify polarity and use a rating ≥6.3 V. A ceramic marked “104” usually means 100,000 pF = 100 nF; with 10 kΩ that gives τ = 1 ms. Recalculate for your value.
  3. Use mode 0 and capture CH0/CH1 at about 100 kS/s or higher. Verify the high and low intervals each exceed 5τ. If not, increase the delay-loop count and rebuild.
  4. Measure CH0-rising to CH1-rising delay. Repeat, compare with the midpoint-threshold calculation, and record both assumptions and result.
  5. Disconnect power and discharge C through 10 kΩ to GND for at least 5τ before rewiring.

Expected: CH1 transitions later than CH0. Compare that delay with your prediction and explain which assumptions could account for the difference.

Bench Guardrail

CH1 reports a digital threshold crossing, not the capacitor’s analog curve. Slow edges may produce jitter or chatter; if they do, record the limitation and use the calculation route. A capture alone cannot determine capacitance accurately without knowing threshold and loading.

Explore an ideal charge curve

Decoupling is also about placement

The Nucleo already has local bypass capacitors. For a later breadboard IC, follow its datasheet; a small ceramic often belongs directly between its supply and ground pins with short leads. A far-away capacitor is less effective because wires have impedance. A 100 nF ceramic near this breadboard’s load is reasonable, but an unchanged digital trace does not prove the supply is noise-free.

Check yourself: if C increases tenfold, what changes?

The time constant and ideal threshold delay increase tenfold; final DC voltage stays the same. Stored energy also increases tenfold:

E = ½CV²
= ½ × 0.000001 F × (3.3 V)² ≈ 5.45 µJ

E: stored electric-field energy (J); C: capacitance (F); V: voltage across the capacitor (V).

This value is for 1 µF; at the same voltage, 10 µF stores ten times as much energy.

Common Bench Trap

Increasing capacitance without extending the high and low intervals may leave charge from the previous cycle, so the observed delay no longer matches a zero-start calculation. Keep each interval above 5τ and retain the series charging resistor.

04 / Inductors · 25–35 minutes · Paper lab

Inductor current cannot stop instantly.

Physical Intuition / Mental Model

Inductor current behaves like the motion of a heavy flywheel: stored energy keeps it going when the drive is removed. If you interrupt its path abruptly, the winding develops whatever voltage the surrounding circuit allows as it tries to keep current flowing.

A capacitor resists abrupt voltage change; an inductor resists abrupt current change. A motor winding or relay coil combines inductance with resistance, and a moving motor also generates back EMF. Keep those effects separate while learning the energy-storage part.

v = L × di/dt

v: voltage across the winding in the passive sign convention (V); L: inductance (H, henries); i: winding current (A); di/dt: current change per second (A/s).

To change current faster requires more voltage; stopping it abruptly can therefore create a large voltage.

E = ½LI²

E: stored magnetic energy (J, joules); L: inductance (H); I: instantaneous winding current (A).

Doubling current quadruples stored energy.

Worked example: a hypothetical winding

E = ½LI²
= ½ × 0.01 H × (0.10 A)² = 50 µJ

E: stored energy (J); L: hypothetical 10 mH inductance (H); I: initial 0.10 A current (A).

The energy remains stored at the instant the external switch opens.

|vaverage| = L × |ΔI| / Δt
= 0.01 H × 0.10 A / 0.000001 s = 1000 V

|vaverage|: average winding-voltage magnitude (V); L: inductance (H); |ΔI|: current-change magnitude (A); Δt: allowed change time (s).

Forcing this current to zero in 1 µs would require that magnitude. A real circuit usually clamps, arcs or breaks down first; this is not a prediction of exactly 1000 V on your motor.

Lab 4 · Follow the turn-off current

  1. Draw supply → coil → low-side switch → ground. Mark current with the switch closed.
  2. Open the switch on paper. The winding current initially continues in the same direction. Without a designed path, energy can charge parasitic capacitance or cause avalanche, arcing or damage.
  3. Add the diode in lesson 5 and draw the recirculating loop.
  4. For a hypothetical stationary 10 mH coil with 100 Ω series resistance, work through the settling calculation below.

τ = L/R = 0.01 H / 100 Ω = 0.1 ms
Ifinal = VS/R = 3.3 V / 100 Ω = 33 mA

τ: RL time constant (s); L: winding inductance (H); R: total series resistance (Ω); Ifinal: steady current (A); VS: applied DC voltage (V).

This stationary coil model has no motion-induced back EMF.

Bench Guardrail

Paper lab only: 33 mA exceeds this module’s external-circuit budget. Your 6 V brushed motor is identified, but its startup/stall current, winding parameters and protective parts are not established here. Keep motors and the relay disconnected; the LA1010 cannot safely characterize an unknown inductive spike.

Real inductors have winding resistance, saturation current and RMS current ratings. Saturation reduces effective L, making current rise more quickly. An inductor’s current limits and a resistor’s wattage rating describe different constraints.

Check yourself: if current doubles, how does energy change?

It increases fourfold because energy depends on I². Motor startup/stall current therefore matters when selecting a driver, even if unloaded running current is small.

Common Bench Trap

Sizing protection from a motor’s unloaded running current can badly underestimate the energy during startup or stall. Establish those currents and a rated recirculation path before moving from this paper model to a motor driver.

05 / Diodes · 25–40 minutes · Build + paper

A diode chooses a current path.

Physical Intuition / Mental Model

A diode is a direction-sensitive current path whose ease of conduction changes with voltage. Across a coil, it can block during normal switch-on and then conduct to provide the return path that stored magnetic energy needs at switch-off.

A forward-biased diode conducts mainly from anode A to cathode K. Its forward voltage depends on current and temperature; “0.7 V” is only a rough silicon model. Reverse leakage is small until device-dependent breakdown. LEDs emit light and require current limiting. Schottky diodes often have lower forward drop, but reverse-voltage and leakage tradeoffs remain.

Lab 5A · Reuse the LED

  1. With power disconnected, identify the LED’s A/K terminals and preserve its 1 kΩ series resistor.
  2. If you have a DMM, disconnect the LED and try diode-test mode in each direction. Some meters cannot drive high-forward-voltage LEDs, so “open” alone is not conclusive.
  3. Restore the known forward orientation and use mode 2. With a DMM in voltage mode, measure the LED drop, then recalculate current. Without a meter, use the 2.0 V assumed example and label it as an estimate.
Bench Guardrail

Identify the LED with the meter or its datasheet, then use the known forward circuit with its 1 kΩ resistor. Do not intentionally reverse-power an unidentified LED to test its reverse rating.

Lab 5B · Add a flyback path on paper

At turn-off, the switch node rises until the diode conducts. The winding then drives a local recirculating loop: current continues through the coil in its original direction and returns through the diode. A simple diode clamp slows current decay and can slow relay release; faster clamps require a deliberate voltage/energy design.

Bench Guardrail

Paper only: connect cathode K to the hypothetical supply side and anode A to the switch side. Reversing them can short the supply when the switch turns on. Select a real flyback diode for reverse voltage, initial coil current, pulse energy and switching speed; an indicator LED is unsuitable. Three-phase inverters need their own recirculation design.

Check yourself: why is the diode normally off?

While the switch conducts, cathode is at +V and anode is near ground. At turn-off, the winding drives the anode above the cathode and recirculation begins.

Common Bench Trap

Reversing the flyback diode makes it forward-biased during normal switch-on, which can short the supply through the switch. Before any later coil build, trace both the ON path and the OFF loop and verify cathode-to-supply orientation.

06 / Bipolar transistors · 35–50 minutes · Conditional build

Switch a load with a small base current.

Physical Intuition / Mental Model

A small base current lets an NPN transistor connect a separate load-current path to ground. For a reliable switch, we deliberately supply enough base current to pull the collector low instead of trusting a typical amplification number.

An NPN transistor has base B, collector C and emitter E. In a low-side switch, emitter goes to ground and collector to the load. Base current turns it on. In the active region, collector current is roughly β times base current, but β varies. For a switch, deliberately provide enough base drive to saturate under the intended load; do not rely on one typical gain number.

Bench Guardrail

Select an identified small-signal NPN. Record its manufacturer’s package pin numbering, voltage/current limits and saturation conditions; TO-92 parts do not share one pin order. If identification is impossible, finish the calculations and sketch and leave the physical lab pending.

Worked design: the same small LED

Assume the conducting transistor drops 0.2 V from collector to emitter and the LED drops 2.0 V. Size the load path first, then the base path.

IC ≈ (VS − VF − VCE(sat)) / RLED
= (3.3 − 2.0 − 0.2) V / 1000 Ω = 1.1 mA

IC: LED-branch collector current (A); VS: supply voltage (V); VF: LED drop (V); VCE(sat): assumed saturated collector-emitter drop (V); RLED: LED series resistance (Ω).

The collector pull-up for the optional analyzer measurement adds another current branch later.

IB,target ≥ IC / βforced
= 1.1 mA / 10 = 0.11 mA

IB,target: target base current (A); IC: intended collector current (A); βforced: chosen collector-to-base current ratio (dimensionless).

A forced ratio of 10 is this exercise’s design assumption; check your device’s specified saturation test conditions.

RB ≤ (Vdrive − VBE) / IB,target
= (3.3 − 0.7) V / 0.00011 A ≈ 23.6 kΩ
IRB ≈ 2.6 V / 10,000 Ω = 0.26 mA

RB: series base resistor (Ω); Vdrive: high GPIO voltage (V); VBE: assumed base-emitter drop (V); IB,target: target base current (A); IRB: current delivered through the chosen resistor (A).

Choose 10 kΩ. The 100 kΩ base pull-down diverts about 0.7 V / 100 kΩ = 7 µA, leaving roughly 0.253 mA for the base.

Lab 6 · An NPN LED switch

  1. Remove the previous external circuits with power off. Wire by the identified device’s pinout. The 100 kΩ base-to-emitter resistor helps hold it off while PA5 is high impedance. A 47 kΩ substitution is acceptable here after accounting for its extra base-current diversion.
  2. Run mode 0. PA5 high should light the LED; PA5 low should extinguish it. The collector moves in the opposite voltage direction.
  3. For an LA collector capture, add the separate 10 kΩ collector-to-3V3 pull-up. Do not rely on the LED branch alone for a well-defined logic high. It adds up to 0.33 mA when on; the example base drive still has margin for this load.
  4. Optional DMM in mode 2: measure VCE, black probe at emitter and red at collector. Compare with datasheet conditions. Excessive drop can mean wrong pins, insufficient drive or a wrong device type.

Expected: LED follows PA5 while collector logic is inverted. The GPIO supplies base current; the transistor carries the load current. This small experiment does not qualify the transistor as a motor driver.

Check yourself: why must the base resistor remain?

The base-emitter junction behaves like a forward-biased junction. Without the series resistor it may draw excessive GPIO current. The 100 kΩ pull-down defines the off state; it cannot replace the series resistor.

Common Bench Trap

Mistaking the 100 kΩ pull-down for the series base resistor leaves the base-emitter junction directly driven by PA5. Keep 10 kΩ in the PA5-to-base path and 100 kΩ from base to emitter; confirm both nodes against the verified transistor pinout.

07 / MOSFETs · 35–50 minutes · Conditional build

Measure gate voltage from the source.

Physical Intuition / Mental Model

A MOSFET gate controls a conducting channel using an electric field, so it behaves more like a tiny capacitor than a steady current-consuming input. What opens that channel is the voltage between gate and source, which means a moving source changes the effective drive even when the gate voltage stays fixed.

An N-channel MOSFET has gate G, drain D and source S. In this low-side circuit, source is grounded and the load connects to drain. The insulated gate takes little steady DC current but must be charged and discharged on every transition.

VGS = VG − VS

VGS: gate-to-source voltage (V); VG: gate voltage relative to GND (V); VS: source voltage relative to GND (V).

Use the source terminal as the reference when judging how strongly the channel is driven.

Bench Guardrail

Threshold is not full turn-on: VGS(th) is specified at a small test current, so a 2 V threshold does not guarantee a good 3.3 V switch. Require RDS(on) specified at 2.5 V or another suitable drive voltage no higher than your actual gate drive, with suitable load and temperature conditions. Typical curves are not guaranteed limits; unidentified parts and parts specified only at 10 V gate drive remain paper exercises. Verify the package pinout.

Worked comparison

Pconduction = I²RDS(on)
= (0.1 A)² × 0.2 Ω = 2 mW
At 2 A: Pconduction = (2 A)² × 0.2 Ω = 0.8 W

Pconduction: on-state heating power (W); I: steady drain current in this example (A); RDS(on): on-state drain-source resistance at the specified drive and temperature (Ω).

These hypothetical currents illustrate scaling, not permission to run them through this breadboard. Switching losses would add to the heat.

Igate,average = Qgf
= 10 nC × 20 kHz = 0.2 mA

Igate,average: average current supplied to charge the gate each cycle (A); Qg: charge needed per turn-on under stated conditions (C); f: switching frequency (Hz).

Peak charging current can be much larger than this average. A GPIO that blinks a MOSFET-driven LED is not automatically an adequate motor PWM gate driver.

Lab 7 · A MOSFET LED switch

  1. Record exact part, package pinout and RDS(on) gate-voltage specification. If the kit has only unidentified or 10 V-drive MOSFETs, complete the paper design and keep the hardware task pending.
  2. Disconnect power and replace the BJT circuit with this one. The 1 kΩ gate resistor intentionally limits the ideal initial charging pulse to about 3.3 mA. It suits slow LED experiments, not general motor PWM.
  3. Use mode 0. LED follows PA5; pulled-up drain inverts it. Use the separate 10 kΩ pull-up when probing the drain digitally.
  4. Optional mode-2 DMM measurements: VGS and VDS. At about 1 mA, VDS can be below a basic meter’s useful resolution. Do not infer an accurate RDS(on) from such a small reading.

The 100 kΩ gate-to-source resistor discharges the gate and defines off during reset. A floating gate can retain charge. An ordinary N-MOSFET’s intrinsic body diode has anode at source and cathode at drain; swapping D/S can create unintended conduction. Verify the datasheet’s symbol and package.

Check yourself: source rises to 2 V, gate stays at 3.3 V. What is VGS?

VGS = VG − VS
= 3.3 V − 2.0 V = 1.3 V

VGS: effective gate drive (V); VG: gate-to-ground voltage (V); VS: source-to-ground voltage (V).

A GPIO’s ground-referenced voltage is not always the gate drive. This becomes central to high-side N-MOSFET switching, which requires a later gate-driver design.

Common Bench Trap

Choosing a MOSFET from its threshold alone can leave it partly on, with excessive voltage drop and heating as load rises. Compare the real gate-to-source voltage with the datasheet’s guaranteed on-resistance conditions and keep this experiment at its small LED load.

08 / CMOS and digital logic · 30–45 minutes · Build + reason

Digital signals are voltages with rules.

Physical Intuition / Mental Model

Digital hardware still carries ordinary voltages; a receiver turns those voltages into symbols using specified limits. A wire can therefore look high to one instrument while remaining an uncertain input to another device.

A CMOS inverter combines pull-up and pull-down transistors. Low input selects high output; high input selects low output. Ideally, steady-state DC current is small, but transitions charge capacitances and can briefly make both devices conduct.

Pdynamic ≈ αCV²f

Pdynamic: capacitive switching power (W); α: average number of charging events per clock cycle (dimensionless); C: switched capacitance (F); V: voltage swing (V); f: clock frequency (Hz).

This model describes capacitive switching power; it does not include leakage or brief through-current during transitions.

VIL defines a guaranteed low-input limit; VIH defines a guaranteed high-input limit. Between them is an undefined region. VOH/VOL specify output-voltage guarantees under stated loads. Noise margin is the gap between a transmitter’s guaranteed output and the receiver’s input requirements. An analyzer threshold is its own decision boundary, not the MCU’s complete input specification.

Lab 8 · Separate level from meaning

  1. Return to the direct LED and external pull-up button from lessons 1–2. Select mode 1.
  2. Write two rows: released → PA10 high → “not pressed” → PA5 low; pressed → PA10 low → “pressed” → PA5 high.
  3. Capture both signals. The code inverts the physical input level even though its human meaning is “pressed means on.”
  4. Compare that with the transistor collector/drain inversion. Software and hardware inversion can cancel depending on which node you observe.

Push-pull and open-drain

Push-pull drives both high and low. Open-drain actively pulls low or releases the wire; a pull-up creates the high state. Compatible open-drain devices can share a line, a principle used in I²C.

Your LM393/LM339 comparators commonly have open-collector outputs, while LM358/LM324 are op-amps; these are not interchangeable CMOS gates. Their supply, common-mode and output limits require part-specific design. A bipolar NE555 is not automatically a suitable 3.3 V logic source. Keep these ICs out of this lesson’s circuit.

Bench Guardrail

Use only 0–3.3 V here. Never join outputs that might drive opposing states. Every device sharing an open-drain line must tolerate its pull-up voltage, including relevant unpowered conditions. The F446’s “5 V tolerant” designation depends on pin type, mode and power state; do not connect UNO 5 V outputs directly. Display modules may contain supply-rail pull-ups, and the kit’s comparators, op-amps and bipolar NE555 need separate part-specific designs.

Check yourself: is the 1.65 V divider a reliable digital high?

No. It may lie in the receiver’s undefined region. A midpoint threshold on the analyzer does not guarantee the MCU agrees. Use the actual receiver’s VIH/VIL limits.

Common Bench Trap

A clean-looking analyzer “1” at a midpoint voltage can hide an undefined MCU input, causing intermittent behavior as noise or temperature changes. Design for the receiver’s guaranteed input limits and reserve the divider midpoint for analog measurement here.

09 / Power supplies · 35–50 minutes · Calculate + inspect

Budget current and draw the return path.

Physical Intuition / Mental Model

A useful supply keeps its voltage steady while different branches ask for different amounts of current. Every branch also needs a return path, and voltage lost along that shared path can move the reference that your signals rely on.

A regulator aims to hold voltage as input and load vary. A resistor divider cannot do that. A linear regulator turns the unused input voltage into heat, while a buck converter transfers energy by switching an inductor and capacitor. The buck’s efficiency comes with the need for a valid control design and carefully arranged current loops.

Worked examples

Ploss ≈ (Vin − Vout)Iload
= (5 − 3.3) V × 0.020 A = 34 mW

Ploss: approximate linear-regulator heating power (W), excluding quiescent-current loss; Vin, Vout: input and output voltages (V); Iload: output load current (A).

At 200 mA the same voltage drop dissipates 340 mW. Package thermal resistance and ambient temperature then matter.

Pout = VoutIout = 3.3 V × 0.100 A = 0.33 W
Iin ≈ Pout / (ηVin)
= 0.33 W / (0.90 × 5 V) ≈ 73.3 mA

Pout: output power (W); Vout, Vin: output and input voltages (V); Iout, Iin: output and input currents (A); η: efficiency as a fraction (dimensionless).

A hypothetical 90%-efficient converter has different input and output currents because power, rather than current alone, is being transferred.

Vout ≈ D × Vin

Vout: average buck output voltage (V); D: switch-on fraction of each period (dimensionless, 0–1); Vin: input voltage (V).

This ideal buck relation assumes continuous inductor conduction and steady operation. It is a starting model, not a complete converter design.

Bench Guardrail

Retain the Nucleo’s documented USB-power configuration. Do not inject power into 3V3, 5V, VIN or analyzer pins, and do not parallel powered boards’ supply rails. Check yourself: why not build a buck converter on this breadboard now?

A useful converter needs a known controller, rated switching devices, inductor, capacitors, compensation, physical layout and analog measurements. The inventory does not yet establish that complete design. Learn energy flow now; use a verified assembled converter later.

Common Bench Trap

A converter assembled from plausible parts can oscillate or produce spikes even while a digital trace looks orderly. Use the energy model for learning and a verified assembled converter with the required analog checks when a later power plan calls for it.

10 / Capstone · 45–75 minutes

A controller you can explain and diagnose.

Starting from an empty breadboard, rebuild the direct LED and pull-up button. Use LAB_MODE 1. Your deliverable is a reproducible wiring plan, predictions, a capture and an explanation of every component.

Acceptance tests

  1. Before power: verify row grouping, resistor values, LED polarity, switch pairs and no direct rail short. If available, use a DMM continuity test with power disconnected.
  2. Released: CH1 high, CH0 low, LED off. Pressed: CH1 low, CH0 high, LED on. Try at least ten presses.
  3. Transition evidence: save a capture with channel names and sample settings. Annotate bounce if observed. One clean capture does not prove debounce.
  4. Current: calculate the active LED and pull-up currents. If a DMM is available, compare the resistor-voltage current estimate with your prediction.
  5. Safe fault exercise: power off and remove only the LED resistor, leaving the load path open. Power on. PA5 should still switch while the external LED stays off. Power off and restore it. Do not replace the resistor with a short or disconnect ground as a live fault.
  6. Reset: press the Nucleo reset button and observe recovery. Describe reset behavior separately from steady operation; GPIO is not configured instantaneously at power-up.

Short oral exam

1. Why can PA5 look correct while the LED is dark?

There may be an open series path, reversed/damaged LED or missing return. Logic switching is not proof of load current. Disconnect power before inspecting.

Common Bench Trap

Treating a clean PA5 trace as proof that the LED branch is healthy can lead you to lower resistance unnecessarily. Power off and trace resistor → LED → return first; a missing series connection is an open circuit, not a request for more drive.

2. Why does a button need both a resistor and software attention?

The resistor defines idle level and limits pressed current. Debounce addresses repeated mechanical transitions. Those are separate tasks.

Common Bench Trap

A pull-up fixes an undefined idle voltage but does not remove contact bounce. If a later program counts every edge, one press may become several events; distinguish the hardware level-setting job from the software timing job.

3. Does an RC delay guarantee perfect debounce?

No. Thresholds, tolerance, contact behavior and ramp speed matter. A robust approach uses an appropriate input stage, often Schmitt-trigger behavior, and deliberate timing. Do not arbitrarily attach a large capacitor directly to an output.

Common Bench Trap

A slow RC edge can linger near a threshold and produce repeated transitions instead of a clean event. Keep the current-limiting resistor, use a suitable input stage, and verify deliberate debounce timing rather than trusting a single clean capture.

4. Why is an LED-capable MOSFET not automatically motor-capable?

A motor can demand high startup/stall current and stores inductive energy. Gate drive, current recirculation, voltage spikes, heat, switching losses and wiring require evaluation.

Common Bench Trap

A MOSFET that stays cool with a 1 mA LED can fail with a motor’s starting current or turn-off energy. Recheck current, gate drive, dissipation and recirculation as a complete power stage before connecting the motor.

5. Which observations need other instruments?

A DMM measures steady voltage/resistance. An oscilloscope with appropriate probes measures ripple, overshoot and analog RC curves. The logic analyzer measures digital timing and protocols.

Common Bench Trap

Calling a flat digital trace a “clean supply” can hide analog ripple and overshoot. Record what the instrument actually measured and leave unmeasured analog claims open until you have the appropriate instrument and probes.
