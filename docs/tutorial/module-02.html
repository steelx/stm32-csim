Lesson 01 / 23

A motor converts energy into motion

By the end: Distinguish torque, speed and position.

The stator stays still; the rotor turns. Magnetic fields produce a turning effect called torque, measured in newton-metres (N·m). Speed tells you how quickly angle changes. Position tells you where the shaft is. A heavy rotor has more inertia: the same torque changes its speed more slowly.

Think of a door: pushing near its handle produces more turning effect than the same push near its hinge. For a perpendicular force, torque = force × distance. A 1 N force at 0.02 m produces 0.02 N·m. Rotation is measured in radians: one revolution = 2π radians. At 600 RPM, the shaft makes 10 revolutions/s, or 62.83 rad/s.

Bench observation · Identify the actuator

  1. With power disconnected, identify the two wires of your brushed motor and the separate power/signal connections on a servo. Do not infer a servo pinout from wire position alone.
  2. Draw three columns: quantity, unit, instrument. Fill in position/degrees, speed/RPM, voltage/V and current/A.
  3. Calculate mechanical power for 0.02 N·m at 600 RPM: multiply torque by 62.83 rad/s.

Record: 1.26 W mechanical output; electrical input must also cover losses.

If it differs: Do not multiply N·m by RPM directly. Convert RPM to rad/s first.

Check yourself: Does twice the speed always mean twice the torque?

No. Torque and speed are separate quantities. The motor’s voltage, current, load and operating limits determine their relationship.

Lesson 02 / 23

The spinning motor pushes back electrically

By the end: Explain startup current and back EMF.

In a brushed motor, brushes and a segmented commutator switch rotor-coil connections as it turns. This keeps useful torque in the desired direction. Rotation also generates a voltage called back electromotive force, or back EMF. It opposes the applied voltage in normal motoring operation.

At steady speed: V = R·I + E, where E = Ke·ω.
Torque τ = Kt·I. At startup ω = 0, so E = 0.

Hypothetical example: a 6 V motor with 12 Ω winding resistance draws about 0.5 A when stationary if inductive transients and brush drops are ignored. Once E reaches 4.8 V, current is about (6−4.8)/12 = 0.1 A. These are invented teaching parameters, not specifications of your motor.

Add load and the shaft initially slows. Lower back EMF allows more current; more current produces more torque. A fixed voltage therefore does not maintain exactly fixed speed. If the motor cannot move, high current can continue heating the winding.

Paper lab · Predict a load change

  1. Use the hypothetical R = 12 Ω, V = 6 V motor. Calculate current at E = 0, 3 and 4.8 V.
  2. Calculate winding heat I²R at each point.
  3. Write why a driver chosen only from unloaded current may be too small.

Record: 0.5, 0.25, 0.1 A; 3, 0.75, 0.12 W winding heat.

If it differs: Do not physically stall the owned motor to reproduce the paper calculation. Obtain its current specification or characterize it with a suitable current-limited setup.

Check yourself: Why does starting require more current than free running?

Initially there is no speed-generated back EMF. Winding inductance limits the initial rise, but the current can rise toward a much larger value than the running current.

Lesson 03 / 23

PWM changes on-time, not pulse height

By the end: Measure frequency and duty on a real STM32 pin.

Pulse-width modulation repeatedly switches a signal on and off. Period T is the time for one repetition; frequency f = 1/T. Duty D is the fraction of the period spent high. A 1 kHz waveform has T = 1 ms. A 250 µs high interval gives D = 250/1000 = 25%.

A transistor used as a switch can spend most of its time fully on or fully off, reducing losses compared with deliberately dropping voltage across it. It still has conduction and switching losses. Motor inductance smooths current; inertia smooths speed. Neither makes the winding voltage constant.

The STM32 output stays near 0/3.3 V as duty changes. A separate driver switches the motor supply. For an ideal winding waveform switching between 0 and 6 V, 25% duty averages 1.5 V. This alone does not specify current or RPM.

Lesson 04 / 23

A half-bridge selects a phase voltage

By the end: Name the two switches and the phase node.

A half-bridge places a high-side switch between the positive bus and a phase node, and a low-side switch between that node and supply ground. With high-side on and low-side off, the node is driven upward. With low-side on and high-side off, it is driven downward. Both off leaves the node undriven by the switches, although diodes and the load can still determine its voltage.

Power MOSFETs need a gate driver. In particular, an N-channel high-side device often needs gate voltage above its moving source. An STM32 pin is a logic command, not a universal high-side gate supply. The bridge diagrams show switching functions, not instructions to construct a power inverter on a solderless board.

Trace · The left leg of the bridge below

  1. Find AH (high side), AL (low side) and phase A in Lesson 7’s bridge SVG.
  2. Write its allowed command pairs: 10 high, 01 low, 00 undriven.
  3. Circle the forbidden 11 pair and follow its supply-to-ground path.

Record: A three-state command table and the bypass path that does not pass through the motor.

If it differs: An undriven node is not guaranteed to be at 0 V; inductive current can drive it to a diode clamp.

Check yourself: What reference defines the motor terminal voltage?

Voltage always lies between two nodes. Phase-to-ground voltage differs from the voltage across two motor terminals.

Lesson 05 / 23

Give one switch time to turn off

By the end: Capture both dead-time intervals.

Shoot-through occurs when both switches in one leg conduct, creating a low-resistance path across the supply. A real switch takes time to turn off. Dead time delays the opposite switch turning on so the previous one can finish turning off.

STM32 TIM1 can generate complementary signals with built-in dead time. In our active-high demonstration both outputs are low during the gap. Adding too much gap also changes the delivered voltage and diode conduction, so it is not a setting to maximize blindly.

Lab B · Capture non-overlap

  1. Use the two-output SVG; the two LED branches are optional visual indicators. Set LAB_MODE = 1.
  2. Capture CH0 and CH1 at 10 MS/s or higher for about 2 ms.
  3. Zoom into PA8 falling → PA7 rising and PA7 falling → PA8 rising. Measure both gaps.
  4. Check that no captured sample has both signals high. Save a zoomed capture of each edge.

Record: About 2 µs each; at 10 MS/s this spans about 20 sample intervals.

If it differs: Check sample rate, channel labels and output polarities. The reference duty is 50%, but active pulses are shortened by dead time; do not expect exactly 500 µs high on each pin.

Go deeper · Why the dead time does not use the 1 MHz counter tick

With 16 MHz timer input and CKD = 00, tDTS = 62.5 ns. In the first DTG range, delay = DTG × tDTS, so DTG = 32 gives 2 µs. PSC = 15 separately makes the counting rate 1 MHz. ARR = 999 gives a 1 ms period. Larger DTG encodings are nonlinear; consult BDTR in RM0390.

Check yourself: Does a clean GPIO capture prove a real inverter cannot shoot through?

No. Gate-driver propagation delays, MOSFET switching times, wiring and faults still need evaluation. This lab validates logic timing only.

Lesson 06 / 23

Turning off voltage does not turn off current

By the end: Trace the inductive off-time path.

Recall Module 1: an inductor stores energy, ½LI². Its current cannot jump instantly. When a bridge changes state, winding current transfers to a diode or another conducting switch. This is recirculation. The winding voltage can change quickly while current remains continuous.

In a low-side switch with a flyback diode, current can circulate through the winding and diode after turn-off. In a bridge, body diodes and deliberately enabled switches provide different paths. Slow decay applies a relatively small opposing winding voltage; fast decay applies a larger opposing voltage. The names describe current decay, not the MCU’s execution speed.

Reason · Follow A-to-B current

  1. In the full bridge, begin with AH and BL on and current from A to B.
  2. Command all switches off: current can initially flow ground → AL body diode → A → winding → B → BH body diode → bus. Energy can return to the bus.
  3. Compare both lows on: A/B are near ground; current circulates through the low-side devices and winding.

Record: A fast-return path and a low-voltage circulation path, with arrow direction.

If it differs: The direction of existing current selects diode conduction. “All gates off” is not the same as “all circuit current zero.”

Check yourself: Why can coast mode still contain current immediately after switching?

Stored magnetic energy needs a path. Current decays through available diode paths before the electrically unloaded shaft coasts.

Lesson 07 / 23

A full bridge reverses the motor voltage

By the end: Distinguish forward, reverse, brake and coast.

Two half-bridges drive the two terminals of a brushed motor. AH plus BL puts A above B; BH plus AL reverses the polarity. These are diagonal switch pairs. Driving both low switches connects both motor terminals near ground and can brake the rotor. Turning all switches off commands coast, after the current transient discussed above.

Reversal while spinning is not a gentle way to stop: the supply and back EMF can combine to produce large current. In our optional motor lab you release the button, disconnect motor power and wait for the shaft to stop before changing direction.

Lesson 08 / 23

BLDC moves commutation into the electronics

By the end: Explain how a three-phase motor differs from your brushed motor.

In a brushless DC motor, permanent magnets are typically on the rotor and coils are on the stator. An electronic inverter changes which coils carry current. The supply is DC, but the winding voltages and currents change with time. “DC” does not mean connect one phase permanently to a battery.

The controller must energize the coils in relation to rotor position. Hall sensors can identify rotor sectors; sensorless systems estimate position from electrical signals. We focus on Hall-based six-step commutation. A complete hobby servo contains its own motor, gearing, position sensor and controller. Sending it a command does not expose its internal commutation.

Identify · Three different systems

  1. Draw your brushed motor as a two-terminal load plus internal mechanical commutation.
  2. Draw the servo as power + command entering an internal controller/gearbox/sensor assembly.
  3. Draw the BLDC as three phase terminals plus optional Hall wiring connected to an external inverter.

Record: Three block diagrams with power and feedback paths marked.

If it differs: Three servo wires are not three motor phases. A two-wire fan may contain internal electronics; here your motor is explicitly identified as brushed.

Check yourself: Can the 1203B drive a bare three-phase BLDC motor?

No. It is a brushed-motor PWM controller, not a three-phase rotor-position-dependent commutator.

Lesson 09 / 23

Count pole pairs before counting speed

By the end: Convert mechanical angle and electrical angle.

Mechanical angle describes the physical shaft. Electrical angle describes where the magnetic pattern is within one repeating field cycle. A rotor with eight poles has four north/south pairs, so p = 4. One shaft revolution then contains four electrical revolutions.

θe = p·θm; fe = p·RPM/60.
Six-step sector = 60 electrical degrees = 60/p mechanical degrees.

Calculate · Hall edges at 600 RPM

  1. Convert 600 RPM to 10 mechanical revolutions per second.
  2. With p = 4, multiply by four: 40 electrical cycles/s.
  3. Count all six sector transitions each cycle: 240 transitions/s. Invert to find 4.1667 ms per transition.

Record: 600 RPM ↔ 40 electrical Hz ↔ 240 sector transitions/s.

If it differs: If measuring only one Hall channel’s rising edges, count one per electrical cycle, not six.

Check yourself: At p = 4, does a 60° Hall sector mean the shaft turned 60°?

No. It turned 60/4 = 15 mechanical degrees.

Lesson 10 / 23

Three legs select a winding pair

By the end: Describe the third phase in basic six-step operation.

A three-phase inverter has legs A, B and C. Each leg has a high-side and a low-side switch. In the basic two-phase-on six-step pattern, one phase sources current, another sinks it and the third is undriven. For A+ / B−, AH and BL are selected; C is floating.

The motor’s windings, not shown inside the switch blocks, complete the circuit. In a star-connected motor, current travels through two windings via their shared neutral. The neutral is generally not connected to supply ground. That difference matters when writing a phase-voltage equation.

  1. Find the selected high-side and low-side switch for sector 1.
  2. Trace bus → AH → phase A winding → neutral → phase B winding → BL → ground.
  3. Confirm both C switches are off and no leg has both switches selected.

Record: A valid path through two windings; the third phase undriven.

If it differs: A colored gate command indicates a requested switch state. It does not show the actual current during commutation or dead time.

Check yourself: Why are six switches needed even though only two phases conduct in a sector?

Each of the three phase terminals must be able to source, sink or stop actively driving as the rotor moves through different sectors.

Lesson 11 / 23

Three Hall bits tell you a sector

By the end: Read H1/H2/H3 without confusing them with phase outputs.

A Hall sensor detects magnetic field. Three suitably placed sensors produce a changing three-bit code as the rotor turns. In the example used here, the six codes are 001, 101, 100, 110, 010, 011. Each adjacent code changes one bit. This is our declared 120° Hall convention; it is not a universal connector or phase-wiring standard.

Some Hall outputs are open collector/open drain and need pull-ups; others actively drive both levels. Their supply voltage does not by itself prove the output is safe for STM32. Our fixture generates known 3.3 V GPIO signals, so no unknown Hall interface is connected.

Lesson 13 / 23

Model one phase, then account for all three

By the end: Understand the terms without memorizing a derivation.

A winding resists current through R, resists changes in current through L, and develops back EMF e as magnets move past it. For a simplified balanced star motor with isolated neutral:

vk − vn = R·ik + Leff·dik/dt + ek(θe,ω)
ia + ib + ic = 0
J·dω/dt = τe − bω − τload

Here vk is a phase terminal voltage relative to supply ground; vn is the star-neutral voltage on that same reference. Their difference is the winding voltage. Leff accounts for the assumed symmetric self/mutual inductance. θe is electrical angle, ω mechanical rad/s, J inertia, b viscous friction. The model ignores saturation and parameter variation.

For nonzero speed, converted electrical power is eaia + ebib + ecic, and torque equals that power divided by ω. At zero speed, use the angle-dependent torque constants times currents; do not divide by zero. Ideal trapezoidal back EMF with correctly aligned rectangular currents can give flat torque, but real commutation takes time.

Calculate · One instant in a winding

  1. Use hypothetical winding voltage 3 V, R = 2 Ω, i = 0.5 A, back EMF = 1 V and Leff = 2 mH.
  2. Subtract resistance and back EMF: voltage left for inductance = 3−1−1 = 1 V.
  3. Divide by 0.002 H: di/dt = 500 A/s. For a short 10 µs interval, estimate Δi ≈ 0.005 A.

Record: Current slope and units; the estimate assumes terms barely change during that short interval.

If it differs: Do not substitute phase-to-ground voltage directly where phase-to-neutral voltage is required.

Check yourself: Why cannot one fixed DC transfer function predict every commutation ripple?

A speed-averaged DC approximation hides angle-dependent phase waveforms and discrete switching. It can help low-bandwidth speed control but omits those fast details.

Lesson 14 / 23

Specify exactly which switch receives PWM

By the end: Explain a six-step unipolar switching convention.

Within the A+ / B− sector, one simple convention chops AH while holding BL on; C is undriven. During on-time, current flows bus→AH→A winding→B winding→BL→ground. When AH turns off, positive A-to-B current can recirculate through the AL body diode and BL. The winding pair sees a small negative diode-related voltage, rather than exactly zero.

An idealized model replaces that off-state drop with 0 V, giving approximately D×Vbus average pair voltage. Synchronous recirculation may instead command the appropriate low switch, with dead time. The phrase unipolar PWM is incomplete unless the switched devices and off-state route are specified.

Lesson 15 / 23

Smooth average speed can hide pulsating torque

By the end: Distinguish switching ripple from commutation and cogging.

During a PWM cycle the current rises and falls. Since current contributes to torque, this can modulate torque too. During a six-step transition, current must transfer between windings; imperfect transfer can produce commutation torque ripple. Cogging is a position-dependent magnetic effect from rotor/stator geometry and can exist without commanded winding current.

Increasing PWM frequency usually reduces current change per cycle for the same winding and switching states, because each voltage interval is shorter. It also increases switching activity and may increase driver losses. It does not automatically remove commutation ripple or cogging.

Lesson 16 / 23

An encoder measures movement one edge at a time

By the end: Decode direction and define counts per revolution.

A quadrature encoder produces A and B signals shifted relative to each other. In our convention, forward progression is 00→10→11→01→00. Reverse follows the same states in the opposite order. One channel alone gives edge timing but not this direction information. An optional index Z marks a reference position, often once per revolution.

x1 counts one selected edge per A cycle; x2 counts both A edges; x4 counts all A/B edges. If the encoder produces 20 A cycles per measured shaft revolution, x4 gives C = 80 counts/revolution. Some sellers use “PPR” and “CPR” differently; write down the edge convention and whether the count is for the motor or gearbox output shaft.

Choose counting time or timing counts

By the end: Calculate M and T speed estimates from the same motion.

The M method counts ΔN edges in a fixed window Δt. It is convenient at higher speed, where many counts arrive per window. The T method measures time Tedge between counted edges. It is often more informative at low speed, but updates only when an edge arrives.

M: RPM = 60·ΔN/(C·Δt)
T: RPM = 60/(C·Tedge)

With C = 80, ΔN = 80 in 0.1 s gives 600 RPM. Adjacent x4 edges spaced 1.25 ms give the same answer. If you time only rising A edges, the count basis is 20, and their interval is 5 ms. Combining that 5 ms interval with C = 80 gives the wrong answer.

  2. Calculate the reported RPMs: 0 and 7.5. Compare a period measurement of 125 ms per x4 edge.
  3. For this example declare a 500 ms stop timeout. At zero edges report “no new measurement,” then “stopped” after the timeout. Explain why speeds below 1.5 RPM may be mistaken for stopped with this timeout.
  4. For a 1 MHz timer, subtract timestamps with unsigned wrap handling. Keep the maximum measured interval below one full timer wrap.

If it differs: Do not keep the previous nonzero speed forever. A narrow timer can wrap before the next slow edge; use overflow accounting or an appropriate counter width.

Check yourself: Which method always wins?

Neither. Window counting trades resolution against latency; period timing trades update rate against edge timing precision and stop detection. Many systems combine them.

Lesson 18 / 23

Measure current through a small voltage

By the end: Calculate shunt voltage, power loss and ADC scaling.

A shunt is a known small resistor in a current path. Measure the voltage across it and use I = Vsh/Rsh. A large shunt gives a larger signal but drops more voltage and dissipates more heat. A small shunt reduces loss but demands more precise amplification.

Vsh = I·Rsh; Psh = I²·Rsh; Vadc = Voffset + gain·Vsh

A low-side shunt sits in a return path, high-side sensing sits near the positive supply, and inline sensing sits in a phase lead. These placements see different currents during recirculation. An amplifier must tolerate the common-mode voltage at both inputs, reject switching transients and settle before sampling. Separate Kelvin sense connections reduce voltage error from load-current wiring.

Lesson 19 / 23

Feedback compares what you want with what happened

By the end: Label reference, output, error and disturbance.

An open-loop command chooses drive without correcting from the resulting speed. Your 1203B knob varies drive; its listing does not establish speed-sensor feedback. A closed loop measures output, compares it with a reference and adjusts drive to reduce the error.

The plant is the thing being controlled: motor and load. The controller computes a command. The actuator/driver applies it within limits. The sensor reports output. A disturbance, such as added mechanical load, changes behavior even when the reference stays fixed. Our sign convention is error e = reference r − measured y.

Think · Become the controller

  1. Imagine trying to hold 600 RPM by watching a tachometer and turning the knob. Write your action when speed is 500, then 700 RPM.
  2. Now imagine the speed display is delayed by half a second. Explain how you might overcorrect.
  3. Identify where a servo’s internal position sensor sits in the same block diagram.

Record: Error signs, corrective action and the effect of delayed information.

If it differs: If increasing drive lowers the measured error in the wrong direction, check sign conventions before increasing gain.

Check yourself: Does closed-loop operation mean the requested speed is always achievable?

No. Supply, current, torque and speed limits can make a reference unreachable; the controller must handle those limits.

Lesson 20 / 23

Use a model to connect input and response

By the end: Read a first-order transfer function and its assumptions.

Start with two graph ideas. A derivative is a slope: how fast something changes. An integral accumulates an area: error of 0.2 lasting 3 s contributes 0.6 error-seconds. A motor takes time to accelerate, so its speed depends on both input and its current state.

Our beginner model is τ dy/dt + y = u. y is normalized speed and u normalized drive; both are dimensionless. τ = 0.2 s sets response time. A step from 0 to 0.5 eventually produces 0.5. After one τ it reaches 63.2% of that change, or 0.3161. This is a synthetic teaching plant, not a measured specification of your motor.

The Laplace transform turns time-domain differential equations into algebraic equations in s. For zero initial state, transforming dy/dt gives sY(s), so (τs+1)Y(s)=U(s). The ratio G(s)=Y(s)/U(s)=1/(τs+1) is the transfer function. Lowercase y(t) is a time signal; uppercase Y(s) is its transform. s has units of inverse seconds and can represent growth/decay and oscillation. It is not another motor voltage.

  2. Double τ. Check that final gain stays 1 while the response becomes slower.
  3. Estimate gain = output change/input change; estimate τ from 63.2% of the final change.
  4. Write G(s)=1/(0.2s+1). Its pole is the denominator root s=−5 s⁻¹; negative real pole means exponential decay.

If it differs: Transfer functions assume a linear model and stated initial conditions. A fitted curve does not capture friction, saturation or every electrical pole.

Go deeper · Derive the DC motor transfer function

Electrical: L di/dt = V−Ri−Keω. Mechanical: J dω/dt = Kt i−bω−τload. With zero initial states and τload=0, (Ls+R)I=V−KeΩ and (Js+b)Ω=KtI. Eliminate I to obtain Ω/V = Kt / [(Ls+R)(Js+b)+KtKe]. R is Ω; L H; J kg·m²; b N·m·s/rad; Ke V·s/rad; Kt N·m/A; Ω is transformed mechanical rad/s. RPM requires the factor 60/(2π). Do not apply this voltage-to-speed ratio directly to position: position is the integral of speed.

A zero is a numerator root; a pole is a denominator root. This physical model can have two time scales. A single-pole approximation is useful only when its omitted dynamics are unimportant over the frequencies of interest.

Check yourself: Is τ = 0.2 s the time to reach the final value exactly?

No. It is the time to reach 63.2% of the total first-order change. The ideal exponential approaches its final value asymptotically.

Lesson 21 / 23

A fast-changing command is harder to follow

By the end: Read amplitude ratio, phase and a Bode plot.

Instead of a sudden step, gently vary the input as a sine wave. A linear plant eventually responds at the same frequency, usually with a different amplitude and phase. Gain is output amplitude divided by input amplitude. Phase lag tells how far the response falls behind in the cycle.

For G(s)=1/(τs+1), substitute s=jω to study a steady oscillation; j²=−1. The amplitude ratio is 1/√(1+(ωτ)²), and phase is −atan(ωτ). Convert frequency using ω=2πf. A Bode plot uses a logarithmic frequency axis, gain in dB = 20 log10(amplitude ratio), and phase in degrees.

At ω = 1/τ: gain = 1/√2 ≈ 0.7071 → −3.0103 dB; phase = −45°.
For τ = 0.2 s: ω = 5 rad/s, f ≈ 0.7958 Hz.

Lesson 22 / 23

Proportional action reacts; integral action remembers

By the end: Tune PI and explain saturation recovery.

P control sets drive proportional to error: u=Kp·e. For the unit-gain plant and Kp=1, holding reference 0.5 needs a steady output of only 0.25: the remaining 0.25 error supplies the 0.25 drive. Increasing Kp reduces that error but also changes dynamics and sensitivity to delay.

I control accumulates error over time. If speed stays below reference, its contribution keeps increasing until enough drive is supplied. Together, u=Kp·e + I, with dI/dt=Ki·e. In the linear Laplace description C(s)=Kp+Ki/s. Ki includes “per second” in this normalized example.

Real commands have limits. If the requested speed cannot be reached, unrestricted integration continues accumulating. This windup can keep drive saturated even after the command is reduced. Conditional anti-windup freezes integration only when the proposed command is beyond a limit and the error pushes it farther out; it permits updates that unwind it.

Go deeper · Exact update order used here

Every Ts=1 ms: read y; compute e=r−y; form Ic=I+Ki·Ts·e. Reject Ic only if Kp·e+Ic > 1 with e>0 or <0 with e<0. Otherwise accept Ic. Set u=clamp(Kp·e+I,0,1), apply any integer-sample input delay, then advance the plant using yNext=a·y+(1−a)(uDelayed−d), a=exp(−Ts/0.2). Initial y/I/delay history are zero. The mechanical model is linear and can show negative y under opposing load; it does not model a static-friction stop.

Check yourself: What happens if firmware adds Ki·error each sample but forgets Ts?

Its effective integral gain depends on the sample rate and can be vastly too large. Ki must be interpreted with explicit units and timing.

Lesson 23 / 23

Correction can arrive too late

By the end: Interpret loop margins without treating them as guarantees.

Negative feedback corrects error, but enough lag can make correction reinforce the deviation. Stability asks whether small deviations decay in the stated model. A bounded motor command or a clipped graph does not prove stable regulation; a limited loop can sustain oscillation.

Analyze the loop transfer L=CGH, not the closed-loop response G/(1+G). At the gain crossover, |L|=1 (0 dB); phase margin is the remaining angle to −180°. At a −180° phase crossover, gain margin tells how much the loop gain can be multiplied before it reaches 1. In dB, GM=−20log10|L(jωphase)|.

G=1/(0.2s+1), C=1+5/s → L=5/s.
No delay: crossover 5 rad/s; PM=90°; no finite −180° crossing → ideal GM=∞.
Delay 0.1 s: L=5e^(−0.1s)/s → PM≈61.35°, GM≈9.943 dB.

Here the PI zero exactly cancels the plant pole: a convenient teaching case, not a robust recipe for an unknown motor. Delay adds phase −ωTd radians. At Td=0.35 s, PM≈−10.27° and this linear closed loop is unstable. Sampling and saturation add behavior not captured by these continuous-loop margins.

Lab F continued · Predict, then run

  1. Press 0.1 s delay and locate the 0 dB crossing in the loop plots. Read the reported margins.
  2. Predict what increasing delay will do, then press 0.35 s delay and inspect the time trace.
  3. Restore PI baseline. Explain why infinite ideal gain margin did not protect the delayed model.
  4. Write the assumptions: negative feedback, this plant/controller, single relevant gain crossover, analytic unsaturated loop. More complex plants with unstable poles or multiple crossings need fuller Nyquist analysis.

Record: Predicted phase loss and observed oscillation; separate the continuous prediction from the sampled, saturated response.

If it differs: The delay buffer here represents transport delay before the plant input. It is separate from the 1 ms control sampling interval; do not count them as identical.

For a first design, phase-margin targets such as roughly 45–60° are starting heuristics, not a guarantee of acceptable performance. Validate rise time, overshoot, disturbances, limits, sensor noise and timing together. The baseline example has a larger margin because it is deliberately simple.

Check yourself: Why should you evaluate margin on the open loop while caring about the closed loop?

The feedback characteristic equation contains 1+L(s). The loop’s gain and phase show how close it comes to making that denominator vanish; the resulting time response belongs to the closed loop.
