Make motion understandable.

A practical route from a PWM waveform to motor models, feedback and BLDC commutation. Begin with things you can see and measure; introduce the mathematics when it answers a question.

What you will be able to do

Explain what makes a motor turn, distinguish motor power from a control signal, capture PWM and dead time, interpret Hall and encoder pulses, and tune a simulated speed loop. Read a simple transfer function without needing a full mathematics course first.

Prerequisites: Module 1’s GPIO/LED exercise, resistor selection, common ground, inductance and flyback. Start with a short refresher on seconds versus milliseconds, frequency versus period, ratios, graph axes and RPM. Explain slope and accumulated area before using derivatives and integrals. No calculus proficiency is assumed.

Pacing: plan roughly twelve sessions of 60–90 minutes, with extra time for reading and troubleshooting. These are study slots, not a completion deadline. The 23 short lessons are grouped into seven blocks; difficult derivations sit behind optional “Go deeper” disclosures. Every topic remains covered in the main explanations.

Experiments to turn the lessons into evidence

These are authoring specifications. The finished guide must expand each into exact wiring, build/flash steps, capture settings, expected readings, troubleshooting and a notebook table. Existing Module 1 firmware does not implement these modes.

A · Capture your first motor-control signal

  1. With USB disconnected, wire STM32 D7/PA8 through 1 kΩ to an LED anode; LED cathode to STM32 GND. Connect LA CH0 to PA8 and LA GND to that same ground. Show exact breadboard rows in the guide.
  2. Flash the supplied PWM fixture; reconnect and capture at 1 MS/s for at least 10 ms.
  3. Measure one period and high-time at 25%, 50% and 75% duty. Predict 1 ms periods and 250/500/750 µs high-times.
  4. Keep duty fixed and change frequency. Explain why brightness alone cannot measure frequency or predict motor RPM.

Evidence: three annotated captures and measured duty. A dark LED suggests polarity/wiring; a missing waveform suggests wrong pin, alternate function, timer enable or capture setup. Physical values follow the board oscillator’s tolerance.

B · See dead time without a power bridge

  1. Disconnect power before adding LA CH1 at D11/PA7. Use logic outputs only; no MOSFET gates or motor are attached.
  2. Run TIM1 complementary-output mode at 1 kHz, 50% reference duty, active-high output polarities.
  3. Capture at 10 MS/s or faster. Zoom into both switching transitions and measure the interval when both outputs are low.
  4. Compare the nominal 2 µs gap against the 0.1 µs sample interval. Check for simultaneous high states in the capture.
  5. Explain why correct MCU timing alone does not prove a real bridge is protected: driver delay and transistor turn-off also matter.

Evidence: two measured gaps. A 2 µs dead time is chosen for visibility in this signal-only lab; it is not a recommendation for an unidentified inverter.

C · Decode rotor sectors with three LEDs

  1. Connect PB4/D5, PB5/D4 and PB6/D10 each through its own 1 kΩ resistor and LED to GND; attach three analyzer inputs to the GPIO ends. Label these H1/H2/H3, not motor phases.
  2. Generate the teaching sequence 001 → 101 → 100 → 110 → 010 → 011, initially one state per second. Fix bit order H1 H2 H3 in code, diagrams and the decoder.
  3. Translate each code into a sector and highlight the corresponding pair of driven phases in the browser. The example mapping is a convention, not a universal motor pinout.
  4. Reverse the sequence. Inject 000/111 and a skipped sector; distinguish invalid code from invalid transition. For this chosen 120° Hall convention, 000/111 are invalid.
  5. Run an accelerated fixture and infer shaft RPM using the known pole-pair count. Compare commanded rate with measured edge intervals.

Evidence: sequence, direction and fault results. The LEDs display synthetic Hall states; they do not demonstrate that a physical BLDC motor commutates correctly.

D · Measure speed without owning an encoder yet

  1. Use a separate GPIO fixture mode on PA8/PA7 to generate A/B sequence 00 → 10 → 11 → 01. Disconnect any servo or other load first. Each state change is one x4 count.
  2. Capture forward and reverse patterns. Decode with the browser’s step-through edge table; include a synthetic CSV so the lab also works without export software.
  3. Define 20 A cycles/revolution and therefore 80 x4 counts/revolution for this synthetic encoder.
  4. Calculate RPM from a 100 ms counting window, then from adjacent counted-edge intervals. Repeat near 6 RPM and at 600 RPM.
  5. Stop the fixture and test a declared timeout; add an invalid two-bit transition. Never report the last nonzero RPM forever.

Evidence: M/T estimates, direction, low-speed quantization and stop handling. Timing measured by the LA is evidence about the fixture, not about a real motor. A kit encoder can replace the fixture after its connector, output voltage, gear ratio and counts are established.

E · Discover a model before learning its notation

  1. Set the browser plant to gain 1 and time constant 0.2 s; step its normalized input from 0 to 0.5.
  2. Read final output and the time to reach 63.2% of that final change. Repeat with twice the time constant.
  3. Write the slope equation, then its transfer function. Work through a matching RC example from Module 1.
  4. Apply a small sine wave around a steady operating point; measure amplitude ratio and lag. Keep it away from saturation.
  5. At 0.796 Hz predict approximately −3.01 dB and −45°. Compare the plotted values.

Evidence: a fitted model, a unit-labeled graph and three frequency-response points. Synthetic parameters are never presented as measurements of your 3 V motor.

F · Make speed recover when the load changes

  1. Start with normalized reference 0.5, plant time constant 0.2 s and no delay. Run P-only with Kp = 1; observe the remaining error.
  2. Add Ki = 5 s⁻¹. Apply a simulated load disturbance and inspect reference, speed, duty, error and integral state on aligned plots.
  3. Command 1.2 while duty is limited to 0…1, then return to 0.5. Compare ordinary integration with conditional anti-windup.
  4. In a separate unsaturated linear-analysis view, add 0.1 s transport delay and check the calculated margins. Increase delay to 0.35 s and observe the loss of linear stability.
  5. Return to the sampled, saturated simulation. Explain why its numerical response differs and why a bounded oscillation does not mean good regulation.

Evidence: before/after traces and a written explanation of steady error, windup and delay. This closes the loop around a browser model; physical STM32 motor PI implementation belongs to the later firmware/control modules.

G · Turn a small current into a measurable voltage

  1. Optional multimeter route: STM32 3V3 → 1 kΩ → red LED → 100 Ω shunt → STM32 GND. The shunt’s upper node is Vsense; measure across the shunt in DC-voltage mode.
  2. Predict current using the measured supply and an estimated LED drop. At 3.3 V and a 2.0 V LED drop, expect about 1.18 mA and 0.118 V across the shunt.
  3. Calculate current from measured voltage divided by 100 Ω; explain deviations from the LED-drop estimate. No ADC connection is required for this first measurement.
  4. In the browser, scale to a hypothetical motor shunt and compare low-side, high-side and inline placement. Mark intervals where a selected shunt does not see the desired phase current.
  5. Add amplifier gain/offset, clipping, ADC quantization and sample timing. Label all analog values as simulated unless measured with an appropriate instrument.

Evidence: calculated or measured LED current, plus an annotated sensing path. The 100 Ω LED shunt is not a motor shunt. No meter: complete the same calculation with supplied example data.

H · Optional: a servo makes the feedback idea tangible

  1. Identify the actual servo, connector orientation, allowed supply, pulse protocol and logic-high requirements. ACEBOTT’s QB054 reference is a lead, not proof that every QE032 revision contains that part.
  2. First capture a signal-only 50 Hz fixture on PA8: 20 ms frame and a 1.5 ms pulse. These are candidate teaching values pending the actual servo specification.
  3. Once compatible, use one unloaded servo on its verified external power path. Connect common ground and the signal; keep the horn free of linkages. The guide must draw power origin and signal origin separately.
  4. Start at the documented neutral pulse and move by small documented increments; do not sweep unknown endpoints. Compare pulse width, duty ratio and observed angle.
  5. Explain that the servo closes an internal position loop. Its signal pin sends a command; it does not supply motor power or return measured shaft position.

Evidence: command capture and observed motion, or signal-only equivalent. Do not assume 1.5 ms means exactly 90° for the owned servo, or that a listed supply range establishes 3.3 V input compatibility.

I · Optional: compare a real motor with the model

Choose one verified path: a suitable low-voltage assembled driver for the identified 3 V motor, an intact documented QE032 motor/driver arrangement, or the 1203B with a motor rated for the chosen supply. Establish connector labels, control interface, starting current and supply capacity before authoring exact wiring. The 1203B remains manual unless an actual interface is documented; do not modify its potentiometer circuit as a beginner exercise.

  1. Record the selected motor, driver and supply specifications in a compatibility table.
  2. Follow that combination’s documented power-up/stop procedure and observe no-load behavior.
  3. Measure speed only if there is a verified encoder/tachometer; visual impressions are not RPM data.
  4. Compare observations with the model and identify missing effects such as friction, supply sag and load.

Actual three-phase BLDC power operation remains a later extension requiring a suitable motor and assembled inverter. The full BLDC learning outcomes here are achieved through diagrams, simulation and signal captures.

Freeze these models and numerical examples

The following are original worked-example specifications, checked arithmetically during planning. They prevent different lessons from silently changing units, counting conventions or plant parameters. Full explanations and interactive calculations come in the guide.

Physical model: keep the units visible

Use L di/dt = V − Ri − Keω and J dω/dt = Kt i − bω − τload. Define every symbol with SI units; ω is rad/s, while RPM = 60ω/(2π). At zero initial conditions and zero load disturbance, derive GωV(s) = Kt / [(Ls + R)(Js + b) + KtKe]. Explain why this is a voltage-to-speed model, not a direct position model. A stalled-current calculation is a paper exercise. R1.

For BLDC, introduce a balanced, star-connected phase model with isolated neutral: vk − vn = R ik + Leff dik/dt + ek(θe,ω) and ia + ib + ic = 0. State the symmetry/mutual-inductance assumptions behind Leff. For nonzero ω, electromagnetic power gives τe = (ea ia + eb ib + ec ic)/ω; use angle-dependent torque constants near zero speed instead of division by zero. A flat ideal torque plot must not be sold as a prediction of the owned motor.

One beginner plant, then explicit complications

Use 0.2 dy/dt + y = u − d, where u is normalized drive, y normalized speed and d an equivalent normalized opposing-load input. This is a teaching model; d is not torque in N·m. Initially u and y are dimensionless and the small-signal plant is G(s) = 1/(0.2s + 1). If an RPM display is later added, declare its assumed scale explicitly.

Details the author must preserve

  • Sampled PI: default Ts = 1 ms. Use e = r − y, form candidate Ic = I + Ki·Ts·e, and reject that integral update only when the candidate command exceeds a limit and the error drives it farther into saturation. Otherwise accept Ic, including unwinding. Then compute u = clamp(Kp·e + I, 0, 1). Plot I and u. Define reset behavior.
  • Numerics: for the normalized first-order plant with held u/d, use exact update yNext = a·y + (1−a)(u−d), a = exp(−Ts/0.2). State controller/update ordering and delay-buffer convention. Keep the analytic continuous loop and sampled simulation separate. Verify full motor-model integration against a smaller time step if implemented.
  • Margins: calculate from loop transfer L = CGH under the stated negative-feedback convention. Explain the −180° crossing and 0 dB crossing. Do not infer universal stability from a positive margin without the model’s pole/crossover assumptions. Report no crossing explicitly, and do not claim an arbitrary finite frequency sweep proves infinite margin.
  • PWM: distinguish a GPIO’s average logic voltage from winding voltage and current. For an ideal bipolar bridge switching +Vbus/−Vbus, Vavg = (2D−1)Vbus. For a defined +Vbus/0 winding-voltage scheme, Vavg = D·Vbus. Coast/body-diode intervals can violate that second approximation; draw the actual paths.
  • Speed methods: M method RPM = 60·ΔN/(C·Δt); T method RPM = 60/(C·Tedge) for adjacent edges under the same counting convention. A same-polarity A-edge period needs a different C from x4 decoding. Specify direction, timer wrap and an explicit stop timeout; explain its low-speed tradeoff.
  • Current sensing: Vsh = IRsh, Psh = I²Rsh, Vadc = Voffset + gain·Vsh. Discuss shunt tolerance, Kelvin sensing, bandwidth and amplifier common-mode range. Sampling must avoid switching transients and respect the valid conduction window. Bus current is not always the same as a phase current.

STM32 signal-fixture contract

Target NUCLEO-F446RE with an explicitly established 16 MHz HSI clock and APB prescalers of 1; do not inherit an unknown Cube clock configuration. PWM: TIM1 CH1 on PA8/D7, AF1; complementary CH1N on PA7/D11, AF1. Set PSC = 15 and ARR = 999 for 1 kHz; reference CCR1 = 250/500/750. For the complementary mode, CKD = 00 and DTG = 32 yield a nominal 2 µs gap because tDTS = 1/16 MHz, independent of the counter prescaler. Include preload/update, output polarity, CC enables and MOE initialization. R7–R9.

The Hall and quadrature generators are separate modes; PA7/PA8 GPIO use is mutually exclusive with their TIM1 modes. Hall uses PB4/D5, PB5/D4 and PB6/D10; preserve SWD and document any debug-pin multiplexing. Servo signal fixture uses 1 MHz counter ticks, ARR = 19999, CCR1 = 1500 for the candidate center pulse. Check Nucleo header mapping against R17 before final diagram generation. No fixture binary is interchangeable with the F411 board or guaranteed to run in this repository’s limited PA5 simulator.

These are freely accessible primary references, not all open-source-licensed publications. Link to them and draw original diagrams; do not copy their illustrations.
